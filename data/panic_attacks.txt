A panic attack is a sudden surge of intense fear or discomfort that peaks within minutes. It can involve a racing heart, shortness of breath, chest tightness, trembling, sweating, dizziness, or a feeling of losing control, even though there is no actual physical danger.

Panic attacks can feel alarming, but they are not medically dangerous by themselves and typically pass within twenty to thirty minutes. Repeated, unexpected panic attacks along with ongoing worry about future attacks may be part of panic disorder.

During a panic attack, slow, controlled breathing can help reduce physical symptoms. Naming what is happening internally, such as recognizing it as a panic attack rather than a medical emergency, can reduce the fear that intensifies the response. Staying in place rather than fleeing the situation, when it is safe to do so, can also help the body learn that the trigger is not dangerous.

If panic attacks are frequent, distressing, or begin to limit daily activities, a mental health professional can help identify triggers and provide effective treatment, which often includes therapy focused on the underlying anxiety.
